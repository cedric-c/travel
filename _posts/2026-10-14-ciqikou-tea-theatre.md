I spent the afternoon wandering through Ciqikou Ancient Town, taking in the old streets, shops, and general bustle.

![Ciqikou street]({{ '/images/ciqikou1.jpg' | relative_url }})
![Ciqikou sights]({{ '/images/ciqikou2.jpg' | relative_url }})

One tea shop caught my attention using the age-old technique of placing merchandise directly into my hand. Sure, I'll try some flower tea.

The shopkeeper and I spent a good twenty minutes chatting in Mandarin while she offered me tea after tea. I told her **我会说一点中文** (*Wǒ huì shuō yì diǎn Zhōngwén*): I speak a little Chinese. She apparently interpreted *a little* rather generously, because she immediately launched into a rapid-fire explanation from which I understood almost nothing.

Fortunately, I had prepared for this exact situation: **我听不懂** (*Wǒ tīng bù dǒng*). I don't understand.

We turned to our phones to fill the gaps, and the tasting was genuinely pleasant. The flower teas were fruity, floral, and sweet. Then came several green teas, all perfectly reasonable candidates for the trip home.

Then I asked the price: **¥1,500** for roughly 300 to 500 grams.

I'm sorry, what?

For perspective, I'm paying around CAD $100 a night for a very nice hotel. I wasn't about to spend several hotel nights' worth of money on tea. Rather than haggle over the unit price, I simply capped my spending at **¥150**, roughly CAD $30. She gave me about 58 grams.

Was this a spectacular bargain? Probably not. But I enjoyed the tea, the conversation, and the Mandarin practice. I could live with thirty dollars for the experience and something to bring home.

![Tea to bring home]({{ '/images/ciqikou3.jpg' | relative_url }})

Afterward, I went looking for food and discovered a small café with a stage. I ordered some **包子** (*bāozi*, steamed buns) and the recommended jasmine tea.

![Baozi and jasmine tea]({{ '/images/tea1.jpg' | relative_url }})

I thought I was settling in for a quiet snack. Instead, the first performer appeared in a mask and started breathing fire. It took me a moment to realize something even stranger was happening: whenever he raised his fan in front of his face, the mask changed almost instantly.

This was apparently **川剧变脸** (*Chuānjù biànliǎn*), the face-changing tradition of Sichuan opera. Very cool, and rather more impressive than my ability to keep up with spoken Mandarin.

![Fire performance]({{ '/images/fire1.jpg' | relative_url }})
![Masked performer]({{ '/images/jester1.jpg' | relative_url }})
![Face-changing performance]({{ '/images/jester2.jpg' | relative_url }})

The next act brought a complete change of pace. A woman in traditional clothing played the **古筝** (*gǔzhēng*), a Chinese zither, and filled the café with a lovely, delicate sound.

![Guzheng performance]({{ '/images/instrument1.jpg' | relative_url }})

Another musician followed with the **琵琶** (*pípá*), a Chinese lute. Her performance was captivatingly serene. Between the music, jasmine tea, and little stage tucked away from the busy streets, the café turned out to be an unexpectedly wonderful place to spend an afternoon.

![Pipa performance]({{ '/images/instrument2.jpg' | relative_url }})

A perfect way to unwind before the *1949* show later that day. I had set out for a little sightseeing and lunch. I came away with tea, twenty minutes of Mandarin practice, a lesson in pricing, and an afternoon of traditional performances.

Not bad at all.
