My first order of business after arriving at my hotel in Chengdu was to find a luggage lock, because apparently hotel room safes are overrated here.

One quick search for "mall" on Apple Maps brought me to what appeared to be a department store. Nine floors of shopping. Surely I’d find what I was looking for.

Or so I thought.

The first floor was pretty standard: shirts, pants, jackets, and the occasional hat stall. No luggage accessories in sight. Fine. Onto the second floor.

More shirts. More pants.

Third floor? Dresses, shirts, jackets, pants.

Fourth floor? You get the idea.

I continued making my way through the building, employing what felt like an increasingly desperate depth-first search, only to discover that all nine floors, each roughly the size of a small Canadian mall, were dedicated to selling some combination of dresses, shirts, jackets, and pants.

**Nine. Floors. Of. Clothes.**

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/stores-2026-10-17.jpeg" alt="Nine floors of shopping in Chengdu" article=true %}

Not a single luggage lock.

Anyway, onto the next department store.

This one looked considerably more promising. Prada, Louis Vuitton, Hermès. Brands I actually recognized.

Surely a luggage lock in *this* building would be affordable.

Jackpot! Second floor. Luggage and travel accessories. Three stalls, two of which didn’t sell locks. Thankfully, the third actually had one.

Now for the million-dollar question.

"Yì bǎi sì shí wǔ kuài (145块)."

Hang on.

145 yuan. Divide by five…

Yeah. No.

Yeeeeaaahhhh, nope.

Definitely not happening.

After searching what felt like half of Chengdu, I had finally found a luggage lock, only to discover that the woman selling it apparently had a distant relative working in the [Ciqikou tea market]({{ '/2026/10/14/ciqikou-tea-theatre/' | relative_url }}).

"Tài guì le (太贵了)."

Needless to say, I did not buy it.

Back at the hotel, I asked Lim, one of the hotel staff members, for a recommendation. Something local. Something uniquely Chengdu.

She recommended two dishes:

> 成都的豌豆面和担担面都比较出名。
> 
> Chengdu’s pea noodles (*wāndòu miàn*) and dan dan noodles (*dàndàn miàn*) are both quite famous.

Pea noodles it was.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/noodles-2026-10-17.jpeg" alt="Chengdu pea noodles" article=true %}

They were pretty good, actually. Unfortunately, I also managed to get oil on my shirt, which meant another trip back to the hotel to clean myself up.

Off to a great start.

After changing and taking a short break, I headed out to explore Chengdu’s nightlife on Siguan Street.

And honestly? Pretty pleasant.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/siguan1-2026-10-17.jpeg" alt="Chengdu riverside nightlife along Siguan Street" article=true %}

I even ran into some Australians getting their ears cleaned by roadside vendors. Yes, apparently that’s a service you can get here in China. Just sit down on the side of the road and let a stranger go spelunking in your ear canals.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/ears-2026-10-17.jpeg" alt="Meeting fellow travellers at a roadside ear-cleaning stall in Chengdu" article=true %}

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/siguan2-2026-10-17.jpeg" alt="Bars and nightlife on Siguan Street" article=true %}

Anyway, there I was, walking down the street, when person after person started approaching me, inviting me in for drinks.

"Píjiǔ! Píjiǔ! (啤酒！啤酒！)"

I wasn’t particularly interested, having already stopped for a drink at a bar recommended to me back in Chongqing.

Now, one thing you should know about walking around China is that there are a few Mandarin phrases that come in particularly handy when people approach you:

* **Wǒ tīng bù dǒng (我听不懂)**: I don’t understand.
* **Wǒ shuō Yīngyǔ hé Fǎyǔ (我说英语和法语)**: I speak English and French.
* **Bú yòng, xièxie (不用，谢谢)**: No thanks, I don’t need it.

That last one is especially useful.

I’d been walking around for a good 15 to 20 minutes at this point, being approached by one person after another. By now, I had the routine down.

"Bú yòng, xièxie (不用，谢谢)."

"Bú yòng, xièxie (不用，谢谢)."

"Bú yòng, xièxie (不用，谢谢)."

Then another guy approaches me.

"Come! Beer!"

And you know what my tired brain decides to do?

"Wǒ tīng bù dǒng (我听不懂)."

That’s right.

After repeating "no thanks" approximately fifty times over the course of this trip, I somehow managed to respond to a man speaking to me **in English** by telling him, **in Chinese**, that I didn't understand.

The poor guy just stood there, utterly confused.

Fair enough. So was I.
