Absolute bonkers-bananas, S-tier theatre.

I don’t even know where to begin.

The music? Nuts. The singing? Nuts. The stage? Nuts. The seating, which rotated independently of the centre stage, which also rotated? Absolutely nuts.

The guy four seats to my left using his phone at 100% brightness in light mode? Also fucking nuts, though for entirely different reasons.

> 中文小注： 英语里有些听起来很负面的词，其实也可以用来夸人或夸东西。比如 crazy 可以表示“疯了”，也可以表示“好得离谱”；nuts 也是如此。
>
> 所以前面几次 nuts 都是在夸这场演出精彩得不可思议。至于那个把手机亮度开到100%的观众？他也是 nuts，不过这次可不是夸奖。
>
> 我以前还用 dangerous（危险的）来形容一道特别好吃的菜，结果对方一脸困惑。其实我的意思是：好吃得危险，因为吃多了对腰围可不太友好。
>
> 至于 bonkers 和 bananas，它们也可以表示“疯狂”。我故意把这些词堆在一起，因为一个“疯”字已经不够用了。

Anyway, back to the show.

Chongqing 1949 takes place during the final days of the Chinese Civil War, as Communist forces advance on Chongqing, then a major Nationalist stronghold. The story follows people caught on opposing sides of the conflict, including underground resistance fighters and political prisoners held in the notorious Zhazidong and Baigongguan prisons.

As Communist forces close in on Chongqing, the retreating Nationalist authorities intensify their persecution of imprisoned Communist revolutionaries and other political detainees, ultimately executing many of them. The production builds toward Chongqing’s capture on November 30, 1949. It’s a story of conviction, divided loyalties, sacrifice, and the human cost of civil war, told from the perspective of the Communist revolutionaries.

Heavy subject matter, to say the least.

![The Chongqing 1949 stage and ensemble]({{ '/images/cover-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Now, back to why I was losing my mind.

Flying performers. Rotating jail cells. An entire auditorium revolving around the action while the central stage moved independently. At times, it felt less like watching a play and more like being physically transported through the story. The sheer engineering involved was impressive enough, but what really elevated the experience was how beautifully everything came together.

![Flying performers]({{ '/images/flying-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

![Performers on and inside the towering cages]({{ '/images/cages1-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The choreography, the lighting, the set transitions, the composition of each scene. Nothing felt like spectacle for spectacle’s sake. Every moving piece contributed to the atmosphere.

![Performers with parasols]({{ '/images/parasols1-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

![Parasols across the stage]({{ '/images/parasols2-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

And the singing. Good God, the singing.

You know those deep, thunderous male choruses that sound like a Russian army marching off to war? That kind of singing. Rich, powerful voices that seem to resonate somewhere in your rib cage. The sort of music that makes you want to stand up straighter and march toward something, despite having absolutely no idea what you’re marching toward.

I don’t know what it is about that sound, but it absolutely hits.

![A singer onstage]({{ '/images/singing1-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

![Another vocal performance]({{ '/images/singing2-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Now, I should mention that the entire production was in Mandarin. I was given an English audio guide, which was helpful in theory. In practice, the bass and treble were so exaggerated that the dialogue seemed to have been sent into witness protection. Between the questionable equalization and the low volume, I struggled to make out much of the narration.

So there I was, watching a historical drama in a language I barely understood, with an English audio guide I could barely hear.

And somehow, I was completely captivated.

I could occasionally recognize a Mandarin phrase, catch the emotional direction of a scene, or piece together what was happening from the performances. But for much of the show, the words themselves were secondary.

![A prisoner shackled to a post]({{ '/images/shackled-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The music, the choreography, the expressions on the performers’ faces, the sheer emotional weight of the production—somehow, it all came through.

![A salute onstage]({{ '/images/salute-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

There’s a French expression I particularly like: « Ça m’a donné des frissons. »

Literally, it means something like “It gave me shivers.” In English, we’d say “It gave me chills.” In Mandarin, there’s a wonderfully physical equivalent: 让我起鸡皮疙瘩 (ràng wǒ qǐ jīpí gēda)—“It gave me goosebumps.”

That’s exactly what happened.

And I think that’s the highest compliment I can give a theatrical production. I didn’t need to understand every word to feel what the performers were trying to communicate. The music and staging carried emotions that language alone couldn’t.

Beautifully orchestrated, technically astonishing, and genuinely moving.

Absolute bonkers-bananas-nuts theatre.

Toward the end, people started taking out their phones, holding them up like lighters at a concert and swaying them gently from side to side. I'd experienced something similar before, but never at a theatre production. There was something unexpectedly beautiful about seeing the whole audience become part of the show.
