After an unexpectedly eventful afternoon in Ciqikou, I headed to the theatre for *Chongqing 1949* (重庆·1949). The performance was scheduled for 4 p.m., and it was a rather dramatic change of pace from jasmine tea and traditional music.

The production takes place against the backdrop of the final days before Chongqing changed hands in 1949, during the Chinese Civil War. At the centre of the story are Communist prisoners held by the Nationalist authorities, particularly at Zhazidong and Baigongguan, and the people caught up in the struggle outside the prisons.

I knew I wouldn't understand every line of dialogue. Fortunately, the staging had other plans.

![The sprawling stage of Chongqing 1949]({{ '/images/atmosphere-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The scale of the production is difficult to convey in a photograph. Performers occupy different levels of an enormous set, with staircases, platforms, dramatic lighting, and entire groups moving in unison. At times, there is so much happening that deciding where to look becomes its own little challenge.

One scene brought a group of prisoners together around a red flag, with people reaching toward it from both sides of the stage. The colour alone was striking, but the collective movement made the moment.

![Prisoners gather around a red flag]({{ '/images/cover-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Other scenes were much more intimate. A prisoner stood with his arms stretched out, shackled to a post. Against the warm orange light and the cooler blue backdrop, the image was almost painterly.

![A prisoner in chains]({{ '/images/shackled-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Then the production would expand again. A line of performers with illuminated parasols transformed the steps into an arrangement of glowing circles, followed by a completely different composition as they spread across the stage.

![Illuminated parasols across the steps]({{ '/images/parasols1-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

![Performers spread out with parasols]({{ '/images/parasols2-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

The lighting did an extraordinary amount of storytelling. Deep blues gave way to reds and yellows, and the set could feel almost unrecognizable from one scene to the next.

![A performer sings under blue stage lighting]({{ '/images/singing1-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

![Another dramatic solo performance]({{ '/images/singing2-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Some moments leaned into spectacle: performers standing above towering cages while others occupied the levels below. The contrast between the people inside the bars and those standing above them was hard to miss.

![Performers in towering cages]({{ '/images/cages1-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

There were also scenes built around military formations and salutes, with crowds filling the steps behind the central action.

![A military salute before the assembled crowd]({{ '/images/salute-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

And then there were moments when the whole space seemed to open up.

![A performer suspended above the stage]({{ '/images/flying-2026-10-14.jpeg' | relative_url }})

Whatever one makes of the production's political perspective, its technical ambition is undeniable. The story is told through a distinctly revolutionary lens, and the historical events behind it are far more complicated than any single stage production could capture. But as theatre, it was a remarkable display of choreography, lighting, music, and scale.

I went in expecting a historical drama. I came out with a camera roll full of images that barely do the staging justice. Not a bad way to end the day.
