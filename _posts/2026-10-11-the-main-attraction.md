Another morning, another breakfast recommendation from Yoyo. When I mentioned my plans to take the train to Chongqing, she told me she'd taken the train there herself but flown back. For reasons. Apparently, the train was worth trying once, but not necessarily twice.

Not wanting to risk a suboptimal experience, I decided to upgrade my seat. China's high-speed trains offer second class, first class, and business class—which, confusingly enough, is the actual first-class experience. At nearly five times the price of second class and twice that of first, business was a little difficult to justify. First class it was.

As it turns out, that decision paid off in a way I couldn't have anticipated. I met someone genuinely interesting along the way, and I'm rather glad I didn't choose a different seat.

But more on that later.

Breakfast was excellent. The soup was savoury without being overly salty, the meal had a good amount of protein, and even the bread was lightly seasoned. The eggs had these beautiful deep-orange yolks, quite different from the pale yellow ones I'm used to seeing in Canada. As for the coffee, every sip felt like the first.

I'll definitely be coming back tomorrow before leaving Hong Kong. Another win for Yoyo.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/breakfast-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Breakfast recommended by Yoyo" article=true %}

After breakfast, I went for a walk and a phone call. You know those conversations where you have to hang up after half an hour, but it feels like you've barely started talking? This was one of those. It ended much too soon, and I'm hoping there will be plenty more like it.

Today's main attraction was the Tian Tan Buddha, better known as the Big Buddha, on Lantau Island. The plan was to take the MTR to Tung Chung, then ride the Ngong Ping cable car up into the mountains.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/plan-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Route to Ngong Ping" article=true %}

Finding the cable car terminal proved slightly more challenging than expected. I managed to get lost looking for the entrance, but at least the detour rewarded me with a rather nice photograph. Getting lost is considerably more tolerable when it produces something worth keeping.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/lost-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="A worthwhile detour" article=true %}

I reached Ngong Ping at around 10 a.m. aboard the cable car, which turned out to be a fantastic experience. Getting the ticket, however, was considerably less enjoyable.

Four failed purchase attempts, two-factor authentication failures, email verifications, the whole nine yards. And all of this while standing in an art museum in Tokyo a few days earlier. Apparently, visiting the Buddha required a preliminary test of spiritual patience.

Fortunately, the journey itself made up for the ordeal. The cable car carries you high above the green mountains, offering sweeping views of the surrounding islands and Hong Kong International Airport, which from up there resembles an enormous slab of concrete dropped into the ocean. Quite the feat of engineering.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/gondola2-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Ngong Ping cable car" article=true %}

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/gondola_view3-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="View from the cable car" article=true %}

As the cable car approaches Ngong Ping, the Big Buddha gradually comes into view. 

At the top, you're greeted by Ngong Ping Village, a collection of cafés, souvenir shops, and even a Starbucks.

Beyond the village stands an impressive traditional Chinese gateway marking the approach to the monastery and the Big Buddha.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/ngong1-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Ngong Ping village gateway" article=true %}

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/temple1-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Monastery" article=true %}

A short walk brings you to a large circular plaza, where the path splits between Po Lin Monastery and the staircase leading up to the Buddha.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/buddha2-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Big Buddha from the plaza" article=true %}

From the plaza, 268 steps stand between you and the Big Buddha. Naturally, I decided to climb them. The ascent was not entirely effortless, although at this point I was beginning to suspect that Hong Kong simply doesn't believe in flat terrain.

As I made my way up, the sheer scale of the bronze statue became increasingly apparent. Standing at 34 metres tall, including its base, the Buddha is already an imposing sight from a distance, but seeing it from directly below is something else entirely.

The statue's raised right hand symbolizes the removal of suffering, while its open left hand represents generosity. An interesting bit of symbolism that gives the enormous bronze figure a meaning beyond its impressive dimensions.

I managed to capture a photograph from below, which really puts things into proportion. Nothing quite like a giant bronze Buddha to remind you how small you are.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/buddha1-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Big Buddha seen from below" article=true %}

And the reward for all that climbing? A spectacular view of the surrounding mountains and the South China Sea, with islands scattered across the horizon. Layers of green hills give way to blue water, and the distant islands seem to fade into the haze.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/buddha_view-2026-10-11.jpeg" alt="Ocean and islands from the Big Buddha" article=true %}

After spending the better part of the morning navigating Hong Kong's transit system, getting lost, and climbing yet another staircase, standing up there looking out over the islands felt like a pretty good return on investment.

Eventually, it was time to head back down the mountain. On the return cable car ride, I found myself passing gondolas full of people heading in the opposite direction, just beginning their own ascent.

One theory suggests that waving hello originated as a way of showing an empty hand, reassuring strangers that you weren't armed. Not that I was expecting anyone to leap across the divide between our gondolas and attack me, but I figured I'd make my intentions clear.
