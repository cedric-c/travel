I started the morning with a noodle recommendation from Lucky. Much like in Hong Kong, the recommendations in Chongqing had been on point. Four Points, actually. That’s where I was staying.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/noodles-2026-10-12.jpeg" alt="Spicy breakfast noodles in Chongqing" article=true %}

Breakfast taught me something about spice that Mandarin classes hadn’t quite managed to convey. There’s understanding the words for “too spicy,” and then there’s taking a quick breath between mouthfuls and immediately coughing because you’ve inhaled Sichuan fumes.

I developed an emotional and gastronomic relationship with the expression that morning.

For my Canadian friends, think of stepping outside on a crisp February morning at −25°C. You take a breath and immediately cough. A bit like that, except the threat is coming from your breakfast.

The biggest challenge, though, was that I’d decided to wear white.

A morning full of bright decisions. Eating those noodles became an exercise in patience: too fast and I’d get oil on my shirt; too ambitious and my mouth and throat would burn. At least it forced me to slow down.

Then I noticed an uncle eating the same noodles, showing the same signs of distress. Sweating, pausing, working through it. I couldn’t believe it. Had I graduated? Was I one of the boys?

Until then, I’d assumed I was the foreigner struggling with the spice. Turns out I was enjoying breakfast and suffering through it just like a local.

The day’s objectives were Fairy Mountain, the Wulong Three Natural Bridges, and the Longshuixia Fissure Gorge. After breakfast, I sent my location to the person organizing the trip and waited for pickup.

I’d already noticed that Mandarin could be quite direct, but this guy took it to another level. He gave the impression that he had somewhere else to be. We were ten minutes into a twelve-hour journey.

The driving was a roller coaster: abrupt stops, rapid acceleration, and what I suspected was a private game of “How close can I get to the car in front without touching it?”

In the van were the people who would become my compatriots for the day: Alois, a German; Jack, a Brit; Jenna and Mia, two Taiwanese girls; and Winnie, a Singaporean. There was also a Chinese couple, though we didn’t get much of a chance to speak.

Our first stop was the Wujiang Gallery, where we paused briefly and I managed to snag a few pictures.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/wujiang1-2026-10-12.jpeg" alt="Scenery at Wujiang Gallery" article=true %}

From there, we drove to a restaurant. The driver had asked whether we wanted lunch.

It was 10:30 a.m.

My mouth was still recovering from breakfast. The itinerary was beginning to feel like someone had squeezed in as many activities as possible without giving much thought to when people might actually want to do them.

We didn’t eat, but I did get to use a squat toilet. Another opportunity for personal growth.

Then we headed up toward Fairy Mountain, and I began to reconsider my wardrobe.

For the previous five days, I’d been sweating consistently, every hour, in practically every place I visited. I’d spent most of that time wishing I’d worn a sleeveless shirt. So that morning, I’d finally acted on this accumulated wisdom.

Sleeveless shirt. Shorts. Mountain excursion.

I’m Canadian. I understand that mountains can be cold. Apparently, I require a practical demonstration from time to time.

I’d checked the weather that morning, but whatever forecast I’d looked at was becoming less reassuring with every kilometre. I’d imagined somewhere half an hour away. We kept driving. Then climbing. Then driving some more.

From the front passenger seat, I’d turned around a few times and noticed how much clothing everyone else was wearing. Back in Chongqing, that had seemed excessive. As we climbed into the mountains, it started looking suspiciously competent.

Thankfully, Jack had packed with roughly the same foresight. By the time we arrived, we were looking at each other and searching for a shop that sold anything we could wear.

We found ponchos for 30 yuan.

Honestly, underpriced. Given my position, they could have extracted a considerable premium.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/poncho-2026-10-12.jpeg" alt="Jack and me in matching ponchos" article=true %}

Fairy Mountain turned out to be an expanse of green pastures dotted with sheep and other grazing animals. Perfectly pleasant, but also familiar territory for a Canadian, a Brit, and a German.

For Jenna, Mia, and Winnie, it seemed to hold more novelty. Fair enough. When you live somewhere like Chongqing, where grass is considerably harder to come by than concrete, I can see the appeal. If you’ve spent time around fields and livestock back home, you’re probably hoping for something a little different after driving all that way.

We did, however, provide some novelty for everyone else.

Already conspicuous as foreign tourists, Jack and I had dressed for the beach and then wrapped ourselves in matching ponchos. The other visitors must have wondered how we’d made it this far without learning to dress for a mountain.

I was grateful that “Canadian” wasn’t written across my forehead. I would have brought shame to my people.

Eventually, we got hungry and spotted a barbecue vendor. A few skewers sounded like an excellent idea. We picked up some extras and offered them around.

One of the girls declined the lamb. She’d just spent time admiring the animals in the pasture and didn’t feel right eating one of their siblings.

I understood the reasoning. I hadn’t given it much thought until she mentioned it.

But had she tried them with Chinese five-spice?

They really hit the spot.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/pastures1-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Pastures at Fairy Mountain" article=true %}
{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/skewer-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Lamb skewer at Fairy Mountain" article=true %}

There was also a vendor selling tea and what I remember being described as rice wine or plum wine. Whatever it was, it had considerably more ambition than the word “wine” suggested. She served it in little shot glasses, which should have been a clue.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/tea-whiskey-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Tea and drinks at Fairy Mountain" article=true %}

Between the skewers and the drinks, the grasslands had begun to make a stronger case for themselves.

Alois and the girls went for a longer walk. Jack and I were ready to come down.

Back at the van, our driver continued to demonstrate his unusual relationship with time. He stopped for fuel with all of us sitting in the vehicle, despite having just had roughly an hour and a half to do it while we were away.

Jack quite reasonably wondered why he hadn’t done it then.

When Jack needed the bathroom, however, the driver honked impatiently for him to come back. Apparently, some stops were more defensible than others.

I still couldn’t decide whether this man was a very good driver or a very bad one. He certainly used the horn with confidence. At one point, as he moved into the oncoming lane near a blind bend, I wondered whether I was about to feature in a news report about a tourist whose driver had decided that saving a minute was worth it.

At least he was honking to warn anyone coming around the corner.

Sound travels in mysterious ways, I suppose.

Then we reached the main attractions: the Wulong Three Natural Bridges and the Longshuixia Fissure Gorge.

This was the landscape I’d come for.

The scale makes you feel tiny. Looking down from above, you can see little specks moving along the paths. It takes a moment to register that those specks are people, and that soon you’ll be one of them.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/ants1-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Visitors on the paths far below in Wulong" article=true %}

The Wulong scenery also appeared in one of the Transformers films. I’ve been collecting photos of Transformers figures around China since my first visit in 2019. They seem to turn up in every major city.

Another addition to the collection.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/transformers-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Transformer figure in Wulong" article=true %}

We made our way down through the scenery by stairs and elevator, taking in the views along the way. Eventually, we became the little specks we’d been looking at from above.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/elevator-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Elevator in Wulong" article=true %}

From down there, the cliffs looked even larger.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/gorges1-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Cliffs and gorge scenery in Wulong" article=true %}

There were shops selling tea at the bottom, which was particularly welcome after all the walking. I’d started the day coughing over noodles, spent part of it worrying about freezing in a sleeveless shirt, and was now happily drinking tea beneath enormous cliffs.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/tea-store-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Tea shop at the bottom of the gorge" article=true %}
{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/tea-store2-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Another view of the tea shop" article=true %}
{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/tea-store3-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Tea shop during our Wulong visit" article=true %}

A reasonably broad range of experiences for one outing.

On the way back, we stopped briefly at a gas station, then later at another one for fuel. At this second stop, the driver had an apparently important phone call to make. It lasted ten or fifteen minutes.

Naturally, Alois and Jack stepped out for a smoke.

When the driver returned, he got in and immediately started driving away.

They were still outside.

He made it a few metres before realizing the van door was open.

After an entire day of rushing us along, honking at bathroom breaks, and treating every minute as a resource of national importance, he’d nearly left two passengers at a gas station following his own extended phone call.

A fitting conclusion to his approach to logistics.

We eventually made it back to our respective hotels and agreed to meet for hot pot later that evening. After spending the day together, it felt right to finish it around a table.

{% include responsive-image.html src="/images/hotpot-2026-10-13.jpeg" alt="Scenery at Wujiang Gallery" article=true %}

Really glad I got to meet this gang. The scenery was spectacular, but the company supplied most of the laughs.

Hope our paths cross again.

Cheers.
